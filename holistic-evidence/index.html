Measured benefit: supporting natural development

PROBIT randomized hospitals to a breastfeeding-support intervention versus usual practice. It did not randomize infants to different vaccine schedules. The infant outcomes demonstrate a measurable, evidence-based opportunity for nonpharmaceutical prevention.

Respiratory infection was 39.2% versus 39.4%, with no statistically convincing difference. View the original randomized trial ↗

What would demonstrate actual “detox”?

A credible trial must identify the compound to remove, measure body burden before and after treatment, demonstrate enhanced clearance versus an adequate comparator, assess organ effects, and measure clinical outcomes and adverse effects. A treatment being described as “detoxifying” within Ayurveda or Traditional Chinese Medicine does not itself establish elimination of a chemical toxin.

Infants: Avoid giving herbal detox mixtures, cleanses, teas, or chelation regimens without a specific medical indication and pediatric supervision. The body's detoxification processes function from birth while continuing to mature. Nutritional care and minimizing harmful environmental exposures can complement, but are not established substitutes for, pathogen-specific vaccination.

Infant nutritionRandomized trial

PROBIT: Breastfeeding-support intervention

17,046 mother-infant pairs in a Belarus cluster-randomized trial. First-year gastrointestinal infections: 9.1% intervention vs 13.2% control; eczema 3.3% vs 6.3%; respiratory infections not significantly different.

Evidence boundary

The trial randomized breastfeeding-support policies, not infants to breast milk versus formula, and not vaccinated versus unvaccinated status.

Infant nutritionInternational policy

WHO: Infant and young child feeding

Exclusive breastfeeding for approximately six months is recommended, when possible, with support for continued breastfeeding and age-appropriate complementary feeding.

Evidence boundary

Breastfeeding has measurable benefits, but specific protection against every pathogen is not established and does not replace pathogen-specific vaccine evidence.

Traditional Chinese MedicineNational survey

China physician survey: Integrated TCM and Western medicine

Of 7,618 surveyed physicians in TCM-related facilities, 84.27% reported integrating TCM and Western medicine in their practice.

Evidence boundary

Physician practices do not reveal that most Chinese citizens detox after pharmaceuticals; the survey did not measure post-pharmaceutical detoxification.

Traditional Chinese MedicineObservational safety study

Singapore: Concurrent herbs and prescriptions

In five TCM clinics, 62.65% of 1,028 surveyed patients reported concurrent Chinese herbal and Western medicine use. Concurrent users reported more adverse events, though most adjudicated events were not attributed to herbs.

Evidence boundary

Selected clinics, observational design, possible differences in illness severity and care seeking. Increased reports do not establish herb–drug causation.

Traditional Chinese MedicineSystematic review

Qingre Jiedu herbal mouthwash and chemotherapy mucositis

A review of 25 articles reported reduced chemotherapy-induced oral mucositis with a traditional 'heat-clearing and detoxifying' gargle in treatment comparisons.

Evidence boundary

Investigators requested larger, standard double-blind multicenter trials. 'Detoxifying' describes the TCM therapeutic construct, not established drug elimination.

DetoxificationEvidence overview

NCCIH: Detoxes and cleanses

Human evidence for commercial detox diets and cleanses removing specific toxins is weak. Liver and kidneys already perform ongoing metabolism and excretion.

Evidence boundary

Specific medically indicated toxin removal and poisoning treatment are different questions from marketed wellness cleanses.

DetoxificationRegulatory safety

FDA: Unapproved chelation products

Chelation can be medically indicated for particular poisonings, but OTC detox chelation has no demonstrated benefit for broad health claims and can cause serious harm.

Evidence boundary

FDA warning addresses unapproved products; prescribed chelation for confirmed poisoning may be appropriate under medical supervision.

AyurvedaEvidence overview

NCCIH: Ayurvedic medicine

Some Ayurvedic approaches have been studied for pain and metabolic conditions, but quality and strength of evidence vary. Certain products can contain toxic lead, mercury or arsenic.

Evidence boundary

Herbal product manufacturing, dose, species identification, and contamination are central to safety. Avoid inferring all Ayurveda is ineffective or unsafe.

Traditional Chinese MedicineEvidence overview

NCCIH: Traditional Chinese Medicine

Acupuncture and tai chi have evidence for selected adult symptoms. Studies of Chinese herbal therapies are mixed, and products can pose contamination and interaction risks.

Evidence boundary

Evidence is intervention- and population-specific; most adult evidence does not establish infant efficacy or safety.

Public family health service cautions against frequent medicinal herbal tea use in infants and young children without medical indications because adverse effects may occur.

Evidence boundary

Safety guidance, not an incidence study. Stronger infant-specific clinical trial evidence would be needed to support any particular regimen.
